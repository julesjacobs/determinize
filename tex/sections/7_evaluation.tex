\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

In this section, we evaluate our determinization approach in three complementary ways. We seek to address the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Exact analysis.} Can determinization enable exact expectation computation through finite Markov reward representations, and what does the transformation cost?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Variance reduction.} How much does determinization reduce output variance?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Monte Carlo efficiency.} Does determinization yield more precise Monte Carlo expectation estimates at comparable sampling cost?
\end{itemize}

Our first question investigates whether determinization enables exact expectation computation through finite Markov reward representations. The latter two investigate its benefits for sampling-based inference, first measuring reductions in output variance and then comparing Monte Carlo precision and sampling time. We detail the benchmarks, experimental setup, comparisons, and results for each question in the following subsections below. All experiments ran on a MacBook Pro M4 Max with 48GB RAM. The artifact contains the implementation, benchmarks, and recorded results.

\subsection{RQ1: Enabling Exact Expectations}

We measure determinization on 15 benchmark programs and separately test exact analysis on concrete instances of those programs. Exact analysis requires a finite Markov reward representation supported by our backend (\cref{sec:implementation}). For the instances in \cref{tab:exact-instances}, Storm/Stormpy~1.14.0~\cite{hensel2022storm} solves the rational reward equations, and Lean's \texttt{storm} tactic checks the resulting return probability and first two moments against the determinized program's semantics using \texttt{native\_decide}. These checks additionally trust the Lean compiler and runtime.

Our benchmark suite draws primarily from prior work~\cite{chakarov2013martingales,chakarov2014expectation,beutner2022guaranteed,barthe2016private,vakar2019domain,sato2019formal,gehr2016psi}, alongside four new examples: \texttt{retransmit}, \texttt{service-policy}, \texttt{workload}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. 
These benchmarks exercise a range of language constructs, including: lists for \texttt{add-noise},
\texttt{gauss-random-walk}, and \texttt{workload}; higher order functions for \texttt{monte-carlo} and \texttt{service-policy}; distributions with nonconstant parameters for \texttt{uniform-nested}; bounded recursion for \texttt{aggregate-rv}, \texttt{dreckon}, \texttt{invpend},
and \texttt{swap}; and unbounded recursion for \texttt{add-uniform-counter}, \texttt{asym-random-walk},
\texttt{pack}, \texttt{retransmit}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. The complete set of benchmark descriptions and programs is~\Cref{sec:appendix-finite-benchmarks}.

\paragraph{Determinization cost.}
The final column of \cref{tab:finite-after-determinization}
reports the total determinization time. This takes less than one millisecond on every benchmark program. Now, to investigate how this cost grows with program size, we

\subsection{RQ2: Reducing Output Variance}

We next measure how much determinization reduces the variability of individual program outputs. Specifically, we measure the \emph{output variance}: how much the values returned by repeated executions of a program vary around their mean. Recall that for a return value $X$, the output variance is:
\[
\operatorname{Var}(X)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\right].
\]
Since $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ is generally not known analytically, we estimate it from repeated independent executions of the program. For $K$ returned values $X_1,\ldots,X_K$, the sampler reports the empirical variance:
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(X)
=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}(X_i-\bar X)^2,
\qquad
\bar X=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}X_i.
\]
We apply the same Monte Carlo procedure to the source program $P$ and its determinization $\det(P)$, with 50,000 executions per program. Rejected observations contribute no returned value, so these statistics are conditional on successful return.

Our suite contains 14 benchmarks drawn primarily from prior literature~\cite{batz2021latticed,moosbrugger2022moment,bartocci2020mora,feng2023lower,beutner2022guaranteed,karimi2022distribution}, with five new benchmarks we added: \texttt{hmm}, \texttt{load}, \texttt{branching}, \texttt{dungeon}, and \texttt{noisy-product}. Full programs are given in~\Cref{sec:appendix-infinite-benchmarks}. We measure how determinization reduces output variance, including for programs that remain infinite-state after the transformation. In some benchmarks, determinization eliminates continuous randomness entirely, while in others it removes only some continuous sampling sites.

\paragraph{Results} Our results are shown in \Cref{tab:infinite-after-determinization}. In particular, determinization reduces output variance on 12 of the 14 benchmarks and leaves it unchanged on the remaining two. The reduction ranges from modest improvements on \texttt{growing-walk} ($1.09\times$) and \texttt{2d-walk} ($1.32\times$) to substantially larger gains on \texttt{dungeon} ($13.02\times$), \texttt{load} ($40.47\times$), and \texttt{hmm} ($108.13\times$). On \texttt{cont-sampling} and \texttt{neals-funnel}, determinization removes all variance in the returned value, giving an infinite variance-reduction factor. On \texttt{vasicek}, the random stopping time leaves nonzero variance, with a reduction of $1.90\times$. In contrast, \texttt{ckd-epi} and \texttt{figure1} show no measurable reduction, illustrating that eliminating sampling sites need not reduce variance in the particular quantity being returned.


\subsection{RQ3: Improving Monte Carlo Efficiency}

A reduction in sample complexity does not necessarily translate into the same reduction in wall-clock time, since determinization may also change the cost of generating each sample. For example, a program may have a large variance-reduction factor but more expensive determinized executions, making its practical speedup smaller than its VRF.  Conversely, even a moderate reduction in variance may yield a larger practical improvement if samples from the determinized program are cheaper to generate. We therefore next evaluate whether determinization improves the \emph{wall-clock efficiency} of Monte Carlo estimation.   We focus on three
representative benchmarks---\texttt{branching}, \texttt{hmm}, and
\texttt{load}---that exhibit substantial but finite variance reductions in
RQ2. 

\input{figures/eval-wallclock.tex}

\paragraph{Results} \Cref{fig:estimator-variance-vs-time} shows lower empirical estimator variance for the determinized programs across all tested sample sizes, with similar sampling times to the source programs. At $N=10^5$, estimator variance is reduced by approximately $5.4\times$ for \texttt{branching}, $105\times$ for \texttt{hmm}, and $24.9\times$ for \texttt{load}. Thus, on these three benchmarks, the variance reductions translate into more precise Monte Carlo estimates at comparable sampling cost.
