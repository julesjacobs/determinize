\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

In this section, we evaluate our determinization approach in three complementary ways. We seek to address the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Exact analysis.} Can determinization transform infinite-state programs into finite-state ones amenable to exact expectation computation, and at what computational cost?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Variance reduction.} How much does determinization reduce output variance?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Monte Carlo efficiency.} To what extent does this variance reduction improve the precision of Monte Carlo expectation estimates under a fixed wall-clock budget?
\end{itemize}

Consequently, our first question investigates whether determinization enables exact expectation computation through finite-state model checking. The latter two investigate its benefits for sampling-based inference, first measuring reductions in output variance and then evaluating whether these reductions improve Monte Carlo precision under a fixed wall-clock budget. We detail the benchmarks, experimental setup, comparisons, and results for each question in the following subsections below. The RQ1 and RQ3 experiments ran on a MacBook Pro M3 with 16GB RAM; the RQ2 experiments ran on a MacBook Pro M4 Max with 48GB RAM. Together with our benchmarks, our implementation is publicly available at \href{https://github.com/[anonymized]}{https://github.com/[anonymized]}.

\subsection{RQ1: Enabling Exact Expectations}

We first ask whether determinization can make calculating exact expectations possible for programs with continuous or infinite-state behavior. For the benchmarks in Table~\ref{tab:finite-after-determinization}, determinization yields a finite-state representation, even when some discrete sampling remains. For these benchmarks, we translate the determinized program into a finite discrete-time Markov chain and use Storm~\cite{hensel2022storm} to compute its expectation exactly as an expected-reward query. Each result is accompanied by a Lean-checkable certificate establishing that the finite model represents the determinized program and that the reported expectation satisfies the exact rational reward equations and boundary conditions.

Our benchmark suite draws primarily from prior work~\cite{chakarov2013martingales,chakarov2014expectation,beutner2022guaranteed,barthe2016private,vakar2019domain,sato2019formal,gehr2016psi}, alongside four new examples: \texttt{retransmit}, \texttt{service-policy}, \texttt{workload}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. 
These benchmarks exercise a range of language constructs, including: lists for \texttt{add-noise},
\texttt{gauss-random-walk}, and \texttt{workload}; higher order functions for \texttt{monte-carlo} and \texttt{service-policy}; distributions with nonconstant parameters for \texttt{uniform-nested}; bounded recursion for \texttt{aggregate-rv}, \texttt{dreckon}, \texttt{invpend},
and \texttt{swap}; and unbounded recursion for \texttt{add-uniform-counter}, \texttt{asym-random-walk},
\texttt{pack}, \texttt{retransmit}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. For details and full programs of each, see appendix.

\paragraph{Results} \Cref{tab:finite-after-determinization} summarizes our results. For each benchmark, we report the queried expectation, whether determinization is full or partial, the number of static discrete and continuous sampling sites before and after determinization, and the determinization time. All 15 benchmarks have their continuous sampling sites eliminated, with seven fully determinized and eight partially determinized. Even when discrete sampling remains, the resulting programs admit exact expectation computation using Storm. These results demonstrate that determinization can enable finite-state exact analysis of programs involving continuous distributions, higher-order functions, and unbounded recursion.

\paragraph{Determinization cost.}
The final column of \cref{tab:finite-after-determinization}
reports the total determinization time. This takes less than one millisecond on every benchmark instance. Now, to investigate how this cost grows with program size, we
take three benchmarks from the table and parameterize them by $k$. For \texttt{uniform-nested}, $k$ counts a chain of dependent uniform sampling sites, each parameterized by the preceding sample. For \texttt{gauss-random-walk} and \texttt{add-noise}, $k$ instead counts the number of explicitly constructed input list elements (see \cref{sec:appendix-scaling} for full details). Increasing $k$ thus enlarges the source program. \Cref{fig:determinization-scaling} shows median times over 10 runs, with interquartile bands.

\subsection{RQ2: Reducing Output Variance}

We next measure how much determinization reduces the variability of individual program outputs. Specifically, we measure the \emph{output variance}: how much the values returned by repeated executions of a program vary around their mean. Recall that for a return value $X$, the output variance is:
\[
\operatorname{Var}(X)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\right].
\]
Since $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ is generally not known analytically, we estimate it from repeated independent executions of the program. For $K$ returned values $X_1,\ldots,X_K$, the sampler reports the empirical variance:
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(X)
=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}(X_i-\bar X)^2,
\qquad
\bar X=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}X_i.
\]
We apply the same Monte Carlo procedure to the source program $P$ and its determinization $\det(P)$, with 50,000 executions per program and seed 0. Rejected observations contribute no returned value, so these statistics are conditional on successful return. Each execution has a limit of 100,000 interpreter steps; an execution failure makes the benchmark fail rather than silently discarding that run.

Our suite contains 14 benchmarks drawn primarily from prior literature~\cite{batz2021latticed,moosbrugger2022moment,bartocci2020mora,feng2023lower,beutner2022guaranteed,karimi2022distribution}, with five new benchmarks we added: \texttt{hmm}, \texttt{load}, \texttt{branching}, \texttt{dungeon}, and \texttt{noisy-product}. We evaluate reductions in output variance on both finite- and infinite-state programs. In some benchmarks, determinization eliminates continuous randomness entirely, while in others it removes only some continuous sampling sites.

For \texttt{2d-walk}, \texttt{bimodal}, \texttt{random-walk-1d}, and \texttt{vasicek}, we replace fixed iteration bounds with a \texttt{flip(1/2)} continuation guard. The number of iterations is geometrically distributed, with mean one and no finite upper bound. These loops terminate almost surely and retain infinitely many reachable states after determinization. Their stopping coins remain random; in particular, \texttt{vasicek} is partially determinized and retains variance from its stopping time. The \texttt{hmm} benchmark retains its fixed sequence of eight observations and becomes finite-state after determinization.

For \texttt{cont-sampling}, we draw the downward-step probability from $\kw{uniform}(1/3,1)$, so the walk moves toward zero with probability $2/3$ at each step. Starting from 10, it terminates almost surely after 30 steps on average. This bias gives a finite source expectation of 5 and variance $5/2$; determinization returns 5 on every terminating run. The unbiased walk has an undefined expectation and infinite second moment, so it cannot serve as a finite-moment variance benchmark.

\input{figures/eval-approx.tex}

\paragraph{Results} Our results are shown in \Cref{tab:infinite-after-determinization}. In particular, determinization reduces output variance on 12 of the 14 benchmarks and leaves it unchanged on the remaining two. The reduction ranges from modest improvements on \texttt{growing-walk} ($1.09\times$) and \texttt{2d-walk} ($1.32\times$) to substantially larger gains on \texttt{dungeon} ($13.02\times$), \texttt{load} ($40.47\times$), and \texttt{hmm} ($108.13\times$). On \texttt{cont-sampling} and \texttt{neals-funnel}, determinization removes all variance in the returned value, giving an infinite variance-reduction factor. On \texttt{vasicek}, the random stopping time leaves nonzero variance, with a reduction of $1.90\times$. In contrast, \texttt{ckd-epi} and \texttt{figure1} show no measurable reduction, illustrating that eliminating sampling sites need not reduce variance in the particular quantity being returned.


\subsection{RQ3: Improving Monte Carlo Efficiency}

A reduction in sample complexity does not necessarily translate into the same reduction in wall-clock time, since determinization may also change the cost of generating each sample. For example, a program may have a large variance-reduction factor but more expensive determinized executions, making its practical speedup smaller than its VRF.  Conversely, even a moderate reduction in variance may yield a larger practical improvement if samples from the determinized program are cheaper to generate. 

We therefore next evaluate whether determinization improves the \emph{wall-clock efficiency} of Monte Carlo estimation.   We focus on three
representative benchmarks---\texttt{branching}, \texttt{hmm}, and
\texttt{load}---that exhibit substantial but finite variance reductions in
RQ2. 

\input{figures/eval-wallclock.tex}

\paragraph{Results} \Cref{fig:estimator-variance-vs-time} shows lower empirical estimator variance for the determinized programs across all tested sample sizes, with similar sampling times to the source programs. At $N=10^5$, estimator variance is reduced by approximately $6.0\times$ for \texttt{branching}, $113\times$ for \texttt{hmm}, and $48.4\times$ for \texttt{load}. Thus, on these three benchmarks, the variance reductions translate into more precise Monte Carlo estimates at comparable sampling cost.
