\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

In this section, we evaluate our determinization approach in three complementary ways. We seek to address the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Exact analysis.} What classes of probabilistic programs can our determinization transformation handle?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Variance reduction.} How much does determinization reduce the variance of program outputs?
    
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Monte Carlo efficiency.} How does this variance reduction translate into more precise Monte Carlo estimates under a fixed wall-clock budget?
\end{itemize}

Consequently, our first question concerns \emph{exact inference}, calculating the true expected value of a program over the exact posterior distribution of the program, while the latter two concern \emph{sampling-based inference}, first measuring how much randomness determinization removes and then asking whether that reduction translates into more efficient Monte Carlo estimation in practice. We detail the benchmarks, experimental setup, comparisons, and results for each question in the following subsections below. All experiments have been run on a MacBook Pro M3 with 16GB RAM. Together with our benchmarks, our implementation is publicaly available at \href{https://github.com/[anonymized]}{https://github.com/[anonymized]}.

\subsection{RQ1: Enabling Exact Expectations}

We first ask whether determinization can make exact expectation analysis possible for programs with continuous or infinite-state behavior. For the original 14 benchmarks in Table~\ref{tab:finite-after-determinization}, determinization yields a finite-state representation, even when some discrete sampling remains. For these benchmarks, we translate the transformed program into a finite discrete-time Markov chain and use Storm~\cite{hensel2022storm} to compute its expectation exactly as an expected-reward query.

Our benchmark suite draws primarily from prior work~\cite{chakarov2013martingales,chakarov2014expectation,beutner2022guaranteed,barthe2016private,vakar2019domain,sato2019formal,gehr2016psi}, alongside four new examples: \texttt{retransmit}, \texttt{service-policy}, \texttt{workload}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. 
These benchmarks exercise a range of language constructs, including: lists for \texttt{add-noise},
\texttt{gauss-random-walk}, and \texttt{workload}; higher order functions for \texttt{monte-carlo} and \texttt{service-policy}; distributions with nonconstant parameters for \texttt{uniform-nested}; bounded recursion for \texttt{aggregate-rv}, \texttt{dreckon}, \texttt{invpend},
and \texttt{swap}; and unbounded recursion for \texttt{add-uniform-counter}, \texttt{asym-random-walk},
\texttt{pack}, \texttt{retransmit}, and \texttt{noisy-iteration}. For details and full programs of each, see appendix.

\paragraph{Results} Our results are summarized in~\Cref{tab:finite-after-determinization}. Determinization eliminates all continuous sampling sites across the original 14 benchmarks, with seven programs fully determinized and seven partially determinized. Importantly, even when discrete sampling remains, the determinized programs still admit exact expectation computation via Storm. This includes programs involving lists, higher-order functions, and unbounded recursion. Thus, our evaluation here serves to show that eliminating continuous randomness can turn a program that is unsuitable for finite-state model checking into one whose expectation can be computed exactly, using a verified finite-state construction and certificates.

\paragraph{Determinization cost.}
The final column of \cref{tab:finite-after-determinization}
reports the time spent parsing, elaborating, inferring types
and sampling modes, and replacing eligible samples by their
means. This computation takes less than one millisecond on every benchmark. Now, to investigate how this cost grows with program size, we generate three benchmarks with increasing numbers of stages $k$ (see appendix for full details). Increasing $k$ adds explicit program structure. \Cref{fig:determinization-scaling} shows median times over seven runs, with interquartile bands.

\subsection{RQ2: Reducing Output Variance}

We next measure how much determinization reduces the variability of individual program outputs. Specifically, we measure the \emph{output variance}: how much the values returned by repeated executions of a program vary around their mean. Recall that for a return value $X$, the output variance is:
\[
\operatorname{Var}(X)
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\right].
\]
Since $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ is generally not known analytically, we estimate it from repeated independent executions of the program. For $K$ returned values $X_1,\ldots,X_K$, we use the sample variance:
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(X)
=
\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}(X_i-\bar X)^2,
\qquad
\bar X=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}X_i,
\]
which is an unbiased estimator of the true output variance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ under i.i.d.\ sampling. We apply the same Monte Carlo procedure to the source program $P$ and its determinization $\det(P)$.

Our suite contains 14 benchmarks, including both full and partial determinization, drawn primarily from prior literature~\cite{batz2021latticed,moosbrugger2022moment,bartocci2020mora,feng2023lower,beutner2022guaranteed,karimi2022distribution}, with five new benchmarks we added: \texttt{hmm}, \texttt{load}, \texttt{branching}, \texttt{dungeon}, and \texttt{noisy-product}. For details and full programs, see appendix. In some of these benchmarks, determinization eliminates continuous randomness entirely, while in others, it removes only some continuous sampling sites but reduces the dimension of the remaining random state. Nevertheless, unlike the benchmarks in RQ1, these programs remain infinite-state and are therefore not amenable to exact analysis with Storm. We therefore evaluate these programs through their reduction in output variance via sampling.

\input{figures/eval-approx.tex}

\paragraph{Results} Our results are shown in \Cref{tab:infinite-after-determinization}. In particular, determinization reduces output variance on 12 of the 14 benchmarks and leaves it unchanged on the remaining two. The reduction ranges from modest improvements on \texttt{growing-walk} ($1.09\times$) and \texttt{2d-walk} ($1.33\times$) to substantially larger gains on \texttt{dungeon} ($13.02\times$), \texttt{load} ($40.47\times$), and \texttt{hmm} ($108.13\times$).  On \texttt{cont-sampling}, \texttt{neals-funnel}, and \texttt{vasicek}, determinization removes all variance in the returned value, giving an infinite variance-reduction factor.  In contrast, \texttt{ckd-epi} and \texttt{figure1} show no measurable reduction, illustrating that eliminating sampling sites need not reduce variance in the particular quantity being returned.


\subsection{RQ3: Improving Monte Carlo Efficiency}

A reduction in sample complexity does not necessarily translate into the same reduction in wall-clock time, since determinization may also change the cost of generating each sample. For example, a program may have a large variance-reduction factor but more expensive determinized executions, making its practical speedup smaller than its VRF.  Conversely, even a moderate reduction in variance may yield a larger practical improvement if samples from the determinized program are cheaper to generate.
