\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Expectation and Moment Analysis}

Several PPLs directly compute or estimate expectations and moments. \textsc{Hakaru} supports calculating exact symbolic expectations via program transformations~\cite{zinkov2017composing}, but does not support higher order constructs. \textsc{Psi} performs exact symbolic inference over discrete and continuous distributions and can answer expectation queries from the resulting exact posterior~\cite{gehr2016psi}; \textsc{$\lambda$Psi} extends this approach with tuples, arrays, higher-order functions, and nested inference~\cite{gehr2020lpsi}, but its loops must be statically bounded and are unrolled during inference. \textsc{Polar} computes exact (higher) moments of program variables by deriving and solving recurrence equations for probabilistic loops~\cite{moosbrugger2022moment}. Its analysis is nevertheless specialized to loop programs and does not provide general recursion, higher-order functions, or recursive data structures. \textsc{Polar} builds off earlier work automatically deriving exact moment-based invariants for the more restricted class of Prob-solvable loops~\cite{bartocci2019automatic}; \textsc{Mora} implements this analysis for expected values, variances, and higher moments~\cite{bartocci2020mora}. Generating function systems such as \textsc{Genfer}, \textsc{Prodigy}, and \textsc{Geni} provide exact moments, including some forms of infinite-state behavior, but operate over more restricted first-order or non-recursive languages~\cite{zaiser2023genfer,klinkenberg2024loopy,li2025generating}. Expectation Programming instead constructs target-aware approximate inference procedures specialized to \emph{estimating} a program's expected return~\cite{reichelt2022expectation}. Determinization is a source-to-source transformation that preserves
expected output and does not increase output variance under the stated
assumptions. It can precede sampling or a supported exact analysis, and
applies to programs combining higher-order functions, general recursion,
recursive data structures, and discrete and continuous distributions.

\paragraph{Quantitative Verification and Invariant Synthesis.}
A complementary line of work reasons about expectations through quantitative invariants and related proof certificates. McIver and Morgan's weakest pre-expectation calculus provides a deductive framework for expectation-based reasoning, but analyzing loops generally requires supplying suitable invariants~\cite{mciver2005abstraction}. This process can be partially automated using templates and constraint solving to synthesize linear quantitative invariants~\cite{katoen2010linear}, or through abstract interpretation to infer linear expectation invariants~\cite{chakarov2014expectation}. Subsequent approaches synthesize restricted classes of polynomial invariants~\cite{chen2015counterexample,feng2017finding}, or construct martingales whose expectations can be related at termination using optional stopping~\cite{barthe2016synthesizing}. Related techniques synthesize supermartingales to bound higher moments of program runtimes and derive tail bounds~\cite{kura2019}, or combine martingales with concentration inequalities to establish probabilistic bounds~\cite{chakarov2013martingales}. Chatterjee et al.\ automatically refute equivalence and similarity of output distributions by synthesizing a distinguishing function together with upper expectation supermartingale and lower expectation submartingale certificates~\cite{chatterjee2024equivalence}. Weakest-preexpectation calculi have also been used to reason about expected runtimes~\cite{kaminski2016weakest} and verify general inequalities over expected values and probabilities~\cite{batz2021relatively}, with dedicated proof rules for lower bounds on expectations and runtimes~\cite{hark2020aiming}. For higher-order programs, Avanzini et al.~\cite{avanzini2021continuation} translate probabilistic programs with unbounded recursion into deterministic CPS terms denoting their expected cost, and use a higher-order logic to prove upper bounds (no continuous distributions).

There are several other works that establish sound upper or lower bounds on quantitative properties of probabilistic programs, as well. Invariant expectations and upper or lower bounds can be learned from program executions without user-provided templates~\cite{bao2022data}. Probabilistic affine forms, higher moments, and concentration inequalities can similarly be used to automatically derive bounds for nonlinear probabilistic computations~\cite{bouissou2016}. Kura and Unno investigate automated analysis of weakest pre-expectations, expected costs, moments, and conditional expectations for higher-order probabilistic programs with continuous distributions~\cite{kura2024automated}. Other approaches automatically derive symbolic upper bounds on expected resource consumption~\cite{ngo2018bounded}. Chatterjee et al.\ derive bounds on expected total resource usage and on the probability that it exceeds a given threshold~\cite{chatterjee2024quantitative}. Further work derives bounds on variance and higher central moments of probabilistic cost accumulators with recursive procedures~\cite{wang2021central}. Classical work on probabilistic recurrence relations derives concentration bounds for randomized algorithms~\cite{karp1994}. Finally, \textsc{GuBPI} computes guaranteed lower and upper bounds on posterior probabilities for higher-order recursive programs with continuous distributions~\cite{beutner2022guaranteed}. Our transformation is fully automatic. When the transformed program admits a finite Markov reward representation supported by our exact backend, it enables exact expectation computation under the soundness assumptions.

\paragraph{Variance Reduction and Randomness Elimination.}
Our approach is closely related to conditional Monte Carlo and Rao--Blackwellization, which replace random quantities by conditional expectations to reduce estimator variance~\cite{owen2013montecarlo,casella1996rao}. Delayed sampling automatically postpones draws and analytically marginalizes variables when conjugate or affine relationships permit, reducing variance during inference~\cite{murray2018delayed}. Recursion can limit the effectiveness of delayed sampling. Subsequent work performs delayed sampling statically when possible, while retaining a dynamic mechanism for programs whose dependencies cannot be resolved at compile time~\cite{caylak2024delayed}. Semi-symbolic inference similarly combines symbolic manipulation with sampling, performing exact inference for closed distribution families and falling back to particle-based approximation otherwise~\cite{atkinson2022semisymbolic}; inference plans further allow programmers to control which variables are represented symbolically or sampled~\cite{cheng2025plans}. In contrast, determinization uses a type-directed source-to-source transformation to replace eligible samples by their means, without requiring conjugacy or symbolic marginalization, while preserving the expected output rather than its full distribution.

\paragraph{Type-Directed Program Transformations.}
Several probabilistic programming systems use static analyses to guide program transformations. \textsc{Slice} uses a type-directed analysis to discretize continuous randomness~\cite{wu2026slice}, while \textsc{GradInf} uses information-flow typing to factor and partially evaluate randomness when constructing gradient estimators~\cite{arya2026gradinf}. \textsc{ADEV} similarly transforms higher-order probabilistic programs into programs producing unbiased gradient estimates~\cite{lew2023adev}. Determinization follows this type-directed transformation strategy, but uses static dependence information to identify random choices that can be replaced by their expectations.
