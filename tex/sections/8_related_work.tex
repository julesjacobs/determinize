\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Expectation and Moment Analysis}

Several probabilistic programming systems directly compute or estimate expectations and moments. Hakaru supports exact symbolic expectation computation via program transformations~\cite{zinkov2017composing}, but does not support higher order constructs. PSI performs exact symbolic inference over discrete and continuous distributions and can answer expectation queries from the resulting exact posterior~\cite{gehr2016psi}; $\lambda$PSI extends this approach with tuples, arrays, higher-order functions, and nested inference~\cite{gehr2020lpsi}, but its loops must be statically bounded and are unrolled during inference. \textsc{Polar} computes exact (higher) moments of program variables by deriving and solving recurrence equations for probabilistic loops~\cite{moosbrugger2022moment}. Its analysis is nevertheless specialized to loop programs and does not provide general recursion, higher-order functions, or recursive data structures. Polar builds off earlier work automatically deriving exact moment-based invariants for the more restricted class of Prob-solvable loops~\cite{bartocci2019automatic}; \textsc{Mora} implements this analysis for expected values, variances, and higher moments~\cite{bartocci2020mora}. Generating function systems such as \textsc{Genfer}, \textsc{Prodigy}, and \textsc{Geni} provide exact moments, including some forms of infinite-state behavior, but operate over more restricted first-order or non-recursive languages~\cite{zaiser2023genfer,klinkenberg2024loopy,li2025generating}. Expectation Programming instead constructs target-aware approximate inference procedures specialized to \emph{estimating} a program's expected return~\cite{reichelt2022expectation}.

\paragraph{Quantitative Verification and Invariant Synthesis.}
A complementary line of work reasons about expectations through quantitative invariants and related proof certificates. McIver and Morgan's weakest pre-expectation calculus provides a deductive framework for expectation-based reasoning, but analyzing loops generally requires supplying suitable invariants~\cite{mciver2005abstraction}. This process can be partially automated using templates and constraint solving to synthesize linear quantitative invariants~\cite{katoen2010linear}, or through abstract interpretation to infer linear expectation invariants~\cite{chakarov2014expectation}. Subsequent approaches synthesize restricted classes of polynomial invariants~\cite{chen2015counterexample,feng2017finding}, or construct martingales whose expectations can be related at termination using optional stopping~\cite{barthe2016synthesizing}. Related techniques synthesize supermartingales to bound higher moments of program runtimes and derive tail bounds~\cite{kura2019}, or combine martingales with concentration inequalities to establish probabilistic bounds~\cite{chakarov2013martingales}. Weakest-preexpectation calculi have also been used to reason about expected runtimes~\cite{kaminski2016weakest} and verify general inequalities over expected values and probabilities~\cite{batz2021relatively}, with dedicated proof rules for lower bounds on expectations and runtimes~\cite{hark2020aiming}. For higher-order programs, \cite{avanzini2021continuation} translate probabilistic programs with unbounded recursion into deterministic CPS terms denoting their expected cost, and use a higher-order logic to prove upper bounds (no continuous distributions). These approaches reduce the manual burden of quantitative verification to varying degrees, but are not generally fully automated end to end: they still rely on identifying, supplying, or synthesizing an appropriate invariant or proof certificate, often within a user-chosen template class or under additional side conditions.

\paragraph{Variance Reduction and Randomness Elimination.}
Our approach is closely related to conditional Monte Carlo and Rao--Blackwellization, which replace random quantities by conditional expectations to reduce estimator variance~\cite{owen2013montecarlo,casella1996rao}. In probabilistic programming, delayed sampling automatically postpones draws and analytically marginalizes variables when conjugate or affine relationships permit, reducing variance during inference~\cite{murray2018delayed}. Subsequent work performs delayed sampling statically when possible, while retaining a dynamic mechanism for programs whose dependencies cannot be resolved at compile time~\cite{caylak2024delayed}. Semi-symbolic inference similarly combines symbolic manipulation with sampling, performing exact inference for closed distribution families and falling back to particle-based approximation otherwise~\cite{atkinson2022semisymbolic}; inference plans further allow programmers to control which variables are represented symbolically or sampled~\cite{cheng2025plans}.

\paragraph{Type-Directed Program Transformations.}
Several probabilistic programming systems use static analyses to guide semantics-preserving program transformations. \textsc{Slice} uses a type-directed analysis to discretize continuous randomness~\cite{wu2026slice}, while \textsc{GradInf} uses information-flow typing to factor and partially evaluate randomness when constructing gradient estimators~\cite{arya2026gradinf}. \textsc{ADEV} similarly transforms higher-order probabilistic programs into programs producing unbiased gradient estimates~\cite{lew2023adev}. Determinization follows this type-directed transformation strategy, but uses static dependence information to identify random choices that can be replaced by their expectations.
