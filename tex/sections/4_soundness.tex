\section{Soundness}
\label{sec:soundness}

Although determinization is a syntactic transformation, its semantic effect can be characterized by conditioning on the random choices that it retains. Intuitively, once those choices are fixed, the remaining randomness comes only from samples that determinization replaces by their means. The transformed program can then return the expected output of the original program under those fixed choices.

This conditional characterization is the key to our soundness result and is useful for two reasons. First, it gives a local account of what determinization does even when the program's overall expectation is undefined. Second, when the global expectation and variance do exist, expectation preservation
and variance reduction follow by integrating over these fixed choices. In what follows, we first formalize this characterization using traces of the retained random choices, and then derive the global consequences.

\subsection{Tracewise Soundness}
\label{sec:traces}

\input{figures/traces.tex}

We begin by making precise what it means to fix the random choices retained by
determinization.

\begin{definition}[$\G$-trace\leanref{traces}]
A \emph{$\G$-trace} is a finite sequence
\[
  \tau \in \Trace,
  \qquad
  \tau ::= [] \mid (D,r)::\tau,
\]
recording the distribution primitive $D$ and sampled value $r$ for each
executed $\G$-mode sampling operation along a successfully terminating
execution.
\end{definition}

When the mode is clear, we simply call these \emph{traces}. Note that $\E$-mode samples do not appear in a trace: they remain random after the trace has been fixed. A trace may contain either discrete or continuous
values, and in the latter case an individual trace may itself have probability
zero.

To reason about outputs jointly with traces, we refine the output semantics as
shown in \cref{fig:traces}.  The measure $\joint e n$ records both the trace and
the returned value of executions of $e$ that terminate after exactly $n$
steps.  At an $\E$-mode sample, the semantics integrates over the sampled value
without modifying the trace; at a $\G$-mode sample, it additionally records the
pair $(D,r)$.  Summing over all termination depths gives a joint measure $J_e$ on $\Trace\times\Real$. Forgetting the output gives
the trace measure $\trlaw e$; forgetting the trace gives the original output
measure $\law e$. These are the two marginals of $J_e$.
Only successful returns contribute mass to these measures. Here, $\trlaw e$ is the measure on traces of successfully returning executions, while $\law e$ is the usual output measure. In particular,
\[
  \trlaw e(\Trace)=\law e(\Real),
\]
so both measures have the same total mass, namely the probability that the
program successfully returns.

The joint measure also lets us speak about the output distribution after a
trace has been fixed.  We write
\[
  \law e(\,\cdot\mid\tau)
\]
for a conditional output distribution satisfying
\[
  J_e(A\times B)
  =
  \int_A
    \law e(B\mid\tau)\,
    \trlaw e(d\tau).
\]
This formulation is important because conditioning cannot in general be
defined by dividing by the probability of a trace: for continuous
$\G$-samples, every individual trace may have probability zero.

When the conditional output is integrable, we write
\[
  \Expect[e\mid\tau]
  =
  \int_{\Real} r\,\law e(dr\mid\tau)
\]
for its conditional mean.  

\begin{remark}[Domain safety\leanref{domain-safety}]
We assume programs are \emph{domain-safe}: almost surely, every executed
distribution has parameters in its domain and every executed division has a
nonzero divisor.  Thus execution cannot become stuck because a primitive
operation is undefined.  We show below that this assumption is necessary.
\end{remark}

We can now state the central semantic
characterization of determinization.

\begin{theorem}[Trace soundness\leanref{trace-soundness}]
\label{thm:trace-soundness}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe. Then $\trans e$ is
domain-safe,
\[
  \trlaw{\trans e}=\trlaw e,
\]
and, for $\trlaw e$-almost every trace $\tau$,
\[
  \int_{\Real}|r|\,\law e(dr\mid\tau)<\infty,
  \qquad
  \law{\trans e}(\,\cdot\mid\tau)
  =
  \delta_{\Expect[e\mid\tau]}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The theorem separates the two effects of determinization.  It leaves the
distribution of retained $\G$-randomness unchanged, while eliminating all
remaining output randomness once that trace is fixed.  More precisely, for
almost every trace, the transformed program returns exactly the conditional
mean of the original program.

The transformed program retains only variation between the conditional means.
The original program's output can also vary after the $\G$ trace is fixed,
due to the $\E$-samples.

\begin{theorem}[Trace variance decomposition\leanref{trace-variance}]
\label{thm:trace-variance}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe. If
$q_e=\law e(\Real)>0$ and $\int_{\Real}r^2\,\law e(dr)<\infty$, then
\[
 \underbrace{\Var_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]}_{\text{Original variance}}
 =\underbrace{\Var_{\mathrm{ret}}[\trans e]}_{\text{Variance between traces}}
  +\underbrace{\Expect_{\tau\sim\trlaw e/q_e}
    \!\left[\Var\bigl(\law e(\,\cdot\mid\tau)\bigr)\right]}_{\text{Average variance within a trace}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

The last term averages the original program's variance after fixing a trace, using
$\trlaw e/q_e$ to condition on returning. It is nonnegative and gives exactly
the variance removed by determinization.

This tracewise statement does not require the program to have a global
expectation.  The conditional mean may be finite for almost every trace even
when averaging those means over all traces does not produce a well-defined
global expectation.  We return to such examples below.  When a global
expectation is defined, however, \cref{thm:trace-soundness} can be integrated
over traces to obtain our global soundness guarantees.


\subsection{Global Consequences}

We now recover the usual global notions of expectation and variance from the
tracewise characterization.

Let
\[
  q_e=\law e(\Real)
\]
denote the probability that $e$ successfully returns. When $q_e>0$, we consider
expectation and variance with respect to the output distribution conditioned on
successful return.

A global expectation, however, need not always be well defined: the positive
and negative parts of the output may both have infinite expectation. We
therefore make precise when the expected return value is defined.

\begin{definition}[Defined expectation\leanref{expectation-defined}]
Let
\[
  r^+=\max(r,0),
  \qquad
  r^-=\max(-r,0).
\]
For $q_e>0$, define
\[
  \Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]
  :=
  \frac{1}{q_e}
  \left(
    \int_{\Real} r^+\,\law e(dr)
    -
    \int_{\Real} r^-\,\law e(dr)
  \right)
\]
whenever the two integrals are not both infinite. Thus the expectation may be
finite, $+\infty$, or $-\infty$; it is undefined only in the
$\infty-\infty$ case. It is finite exactly when
\[
  \int_{\Real}|r|\,\law e(dr)<\infty.
\]
\end{definition}

When the second moment is finite, we define the return-conditioned variance in
the usual way:
\[
  \Var_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]
  =
  \frac1{q_e}\int_{\Real}
  \bigl(r-\Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]\bigr)^2\,\law e(dr).
\]
This measures the variability of returned values around their conditional
mean, among executions that successfully return. With these notions in place, trace soundness yields the corresponding global
guarantees.

\begin{theorem}[Expectation preservation\leanref{expectation-preservation}]
\label{thm:expectation}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe.

First, determinization preserves the probability of successful return:
\[
  \law{\trans e}(\Real)
  =
  \law e(\Real).
  \leanref{output-mass}
\]

If $q_e>0$ and $\Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]$ is defined, then the transformed
program also has a defined expectation and
\[
  \Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[\trans e]
  =
  \Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[e].
  \leanref{expectation-preservation}
\]
\end{theorem}

The expectation statement is the law of total expectation: determinization
replaces each conditional output distribution by its mean and then averages
over the same distribution of traces.  The variance statement is the
corresponding law of total variance.  Determinization preserves variation
between different $\G$-traces and removes exactly the variation that remains
after a trace has been fixed.

\begin{theorem}[Variance non-increase\leanref{variance-non-increase}]
\label{thm:variance}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe. If
$\law e(\Real)>0$ and $\int_{\Real}r^2\,\law e(dr)<\infty$, then $\trans e$
also has positive probability of returning and a finite second moment, and
\[
 \Var_{\mathrm{ret}}[\trans e]\le\Var_{\mathrm{ret}}[e].
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Convex-function inequality]
\label{thm:convex-function}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe. Then, for every
nonnegative convex function $\varphi:\Real\to\Real$,
\[
  \int_{\Real}
    \varphi(r)\,\law{\trans e}(dr)
  \leq
  \int_{\Real}
    \varphi(r)\,\law e(dr),
\]
where both sides are understood as nonnegative extended integrals and may
therefore equal $+\infty$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Divergence and rejection.}
Rejection, as in a failed $\kw{observe}$, contributes no output; we model
$\kw{reject}$ as an infinite loop.  Let $d_e$ denote the probability of
divergence\leanref{divergence}, including rejection.  Under domain safety,
executions cannot lose probability mass by getting stuck, and hence
\[
  \law e(\Real)+d_e=1.
  \leanref{return-or-diverge}
\]
Together with preservation of output mass, this implies that determinization
also preserves the total probability of divergence and rejection.

\begin{proposition}[Probability preservation]
\label{prop:probability}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe. Then $\trans e$ is domain-safe,
and
\[
 \law e(\Real)+d_e=1\leanref{return-or-diverge},
 \qquad
 \law{\trans e}(\Real)=\law e(\Real)\leanref{output-mass}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Undefined global expectations.}
The tracewise theorem is strictly more general than the expectation statement
of \cref{thm:expectation}.  Consider a program that flips a fair coin
until tails, then samples uniformly from $[-2^n,2^n]$, where $n$ is the
number of preceding heads:
\begin{lstlisting}
let sample = rec f bound =>
  if bernoulli[G]\(1/2) < 1 then uniform[E]\(-bound, bound)
  else f (2 \* bound)
in sample 1
\end{lstlisting}
After $n$ heads followed by tails, the remaining $\E$-sample is
$\draw{uniform}\E{-2^n,2^n}$, whose conditional mean is zero.  Thus
determinization returns zero on every terminating trace.

Globally, however, if $N$ is the number of heads and $X$ the returned value,
then
\[
  \Pr(N=n)=2^{-n-1},
  \qquad
  \Expect[|X|]
  =
  \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
  2^{-n-1}\frac{2^n}{2}
  =
  \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac14
  =
  \infty.
\]
By symmetry,
\[
  \Expect[X^+]=\Expect[X^-]=\infty,
\]
so $\Expect[X]$ is undefined according to the definition above.  Hence global
expectation preservation cannot even be stated for this program, while
\cref{thm:trace-soundness} still characterizes exactly what determinization
does on almost every terminating trace.

This distinction also matters for continuous traces.  For example,
\begin{lstlisting}
let u = uniform[G]\(-1, 1) in
uniform[E]\(0, 2) / u
\end{lstlisting}
has an undefined global expectation, but for almost every fixed $u$ the
conditional mean is $1/u$, exactly the value returned by the determinized
program.  Because every individual value of $u$ has probability zero,
conditioning must be understood through the joint measure $J_e$, rather than
by dividing by the probability of a particular trace.

\paragraph{Why domain safety is necessary.}
Without domain safety, determinization can change which executions
successfully return.  For example,
\begin{lstlisting}
let u = gaussian[E]\(0, 1) in
uniform[E]\(0, u)
\end{lstlisting}
is not domain-safe: executions with $u<0$ become stuck.  Conditioning on
successful return therefore implicitly conditions $u$ to be positive, giving
\[
  \Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[e]
  =
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.
\]
Determinization instead sets $u$ to its unconditional mean $0$, yielding
\[
  \Expect_{\mathrm{ret}}[\trans e]=0.
\]
Thus the domain-safety assumption prevents determinization from changing the
set of semantically valid executions.

The next section proves these results by introducing a symbolic semantics that
delays $\E$-mode samples while executing $\G$-mode samples normally.  The key
argument shows that symbolic execution commutes both with sampling the delayed
variables and with replacing them by their means; trace soundness and the
global results above then follow.
