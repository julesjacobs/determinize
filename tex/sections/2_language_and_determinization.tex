\section{Language and Determinization}
\label{sec:language}

This section introduces the probabilistic language on which our determinization transformation operates. We present its syntax and operational semantics, define determinization as a source-to-source transformation over annotated expressions, and formalize the distribution of successfully returned values through an output measure.

\noindent recursion, pairs, sums, lists, and probability distributions. The expression $\kw{reject}$ represents a failed execution path used to encode conditioning on observations. Each distribution expression carries a \emph{distribution mode} $\G$, $\E$, or $\M$ that guides determinization, as illustrated by the examples in~\Cref{sec:introduction}. The modes $\G$ and $\E$ mark sampling sites to preserve or replace by mean evaluations, respectively. Determinization changes $\E$ to $\M$, which evaluates the distribution expression to its mean. We present these modes explicitly in the syntax to define the transformation precisely, but users may omit them: the inference procedure in \cref{sec:type-system} assigns these modes subject to the typing rules, which check that the replacements are justified by how sampled values are subsequently used.


\paragraph{Operational semantics.}
\Cref{fig:semantics} gives the call-by-value operational semantics.
Values and evaluation contexts are shown in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-values}). In particular, a distribution's arguments are evaluated to values before it is sampled or its mean is computed.

The deterministic reduction rules are shown in \cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-reduction}). We omit the
routine arithmetic and comparison rules here. We model the expression $\kw{reject}$ as divergence through the infinite reduction sequence $\kw{reject}\rightsquigarrow\kw{reject}$, and thus our semantics does not distinguish rejection from nontermination. Distribution expressions in
mode $\M$, introduced by determinization, also reduce deterministically
to their means:
\(
  D^\M(\bar v)
  \rightsquigarrow
  \operatorname{Mean}_D(\bar v)
  \qquad
  \text{when }\operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v),
\) 
where the primitive distribution table in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-distributions}) specifies
$\operatorname{Domain}_D$ and $\operatorname{Mean}_D$ for each supported
distribution. A distribution expression whose arguments lie outside its
domain becomes stuck, as does division by zero: execution cannot take another step or return a value. In $\kw{gaussian}(u,v)$, $v$ denotes
the variance; the parameters $\lambda$ of the exponential and gamma
distributions are rates. The distribution
$\kw{discrete}([p_i]_{i<n})$ returns $i$ with probability $p_i$ for
$0\leq i<n$, and returns $n$ with the remaining probability
$1-\sum_{i<n}p_i$.

In contrast to mode $\M$, distribution expressions with modes $\G$ and $\E$ both sample from
$D(\bar v)$ when the arguments satisfy the domain condition $\operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v)$.
Since sampling is stochastic, neither mode has a deterministic reduction
rule. Their identical sampling behavior is instead specified by the
output measure rules in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-output}), which we describe
later in this section.

\paragraph{Determinization} We now define the determinization transformation, with the full inductive definition deferred to \Cref{sec:appendix-determinization}. 

\begin{definition}[Determinization\leanref{determinization}]
\label{def:determinization}
Let $\mathcal{A}=\{\G,\E,\M\}$ be the set of distribution modes. The
determinization transformation $\trans{\cdot}$ is defined structurally on
expressions, changing each $\E$-mode distribution to $\M$-mode and leaving all other constructs unchanged. In particular, for
every distribution $D$, mode $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}$, and argument
expressions $\bar e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\[
\trans{D^\alpha(\bar e)}
=
\begin{cases}
D^\M(\trans{\bar e}) & \text{if } \alpha=\E,\\
D^\alpha(\trans{\bar e}) & \text{if } \alpha\in\{\G,\M\},
\end{cases}
\]
where
\[
\trans{\bar e}
=
(\trans{e_1},\ldots,\trans{e_n}).
\]
For every non-distribution constructor $C$,
\[
\trans{C(e_1,\ldots,e_n)}
=
C(\trans{e_1},\ldots,\trans{e_n}),
\]
and atomic expressions are unchanged.
\end{definition}

Most notably, determinization replaces an $\E$-mode distribution with its mean evaluation and recursively transforms the distribution's arguments, preserving their call-by-value evaluation order. When all sampling sites carry distribution mode $\E$, determinization eliminates all randomness, yielding a deterministic program. We refer to this as \emph{full determinization}. When both $\E$- and $\G$-mode distributions occur, determinization eliminates randomness in the former while retaining randomness in the latter. Accordingly, we refer to this as \emph{partial determinization}. In this case, the resulting program may be discrete or remain continuous, depending on the randomness retained. In particular, if all retained sampling sites are discrete, the transformed program is discrete, even if the original program contains continuous distributions. Conversely, retaining continuous sampling sites may leave the transformed program continuous. \Cref{sec:appendix-determinization-examples} gives several simple examples to illustrate full and partial determinization in action.

We note that determinization itself is syntactic: it performs the required replacements, but the type system is what establishes that those replacements are semantically justified. For example, the type system prevents the $\kw{uniform}$ distribution in
\[
  \kw{let}\ x=\kw{uniform}^{\E}(0,1)\ \kw{in}\ x*x
\]
from being assigned mode $\E$, since for $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$, the original program returns $X^2$, with
expectation $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac{1}{3}$, but replacing $X$ by its mean
instead returns $\mathbb{E}[X]^2=\frac{1}{4}$, changing the expected
returned value. We formalize such constraints in \cref{sec:type-system}.

\paragraph{Output measures.}
To characterize the probabilistic behavior of a program,
we define a subprobability measure over \emph{returned real values}, as shown in \cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-output}). Here, $\delta_r$ denotes the Dirac measure at $r$, defined by $\delta_r(B)=1$ if $r\in B$ and $0$ otherwise. We equip $\mathbb R$ with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra and let
$\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$ denote the set of subprobability measures on this measurable space. Let $Expr$ be the set of all expressions. For each $n\in\mathbb N$, the function
$\out{(\cdot)}{n}\colon Expr\to\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$
assigns each expression $e$ its output measure after exactly $n$
reduction steps. 
Summing over all finite $n$ defines the function
$\law{(\cdot)}\colon Expr\to\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$,
which assigns each expression its complete output measure.
For each Borel set $B\subseteq\mathbb R$, $\out e n(B)$ and $\law e(B)$
are the probabilities that $e$ returns a value in $B$ after exactly
$n$ steps and after any finite number of steps, respectively.
Since only executions that successfully return a real value
contribute mass, $\law e(\Real)\leq 1$, with the missing mass
accounting for rejection, divergence, stuck executions, or returns of non-real values.
Thus, $\law e(\Real)$ is precisely the probability that
$e$ successfully returns a real value.

Although executions that reject, diverge, or become stuck contribute no mass to the output measure, stuck executions require special consideration. Replacing a random sample by its mean may change whether a subsequent operation is defined. For example, a uniform distribution whose upper bound depends on a random sample may be undefined for some sampled values but become well-defined after determinization, which can alter the probability of successful return (see~\Cref{sec:appendix-misc}). So to rule out such behavior, we introduce the following
domain safety condition, and henceforth assume that all programs we consider are domain safe.

\begin{definition}[Domain safety\leanref{domain-safety}]
Define $\mathsf{Safe}_n(e)$ recursively on $n$.
Set $\mathsf{Safe}_0(e)=\top$ for every expression $e$.
For $n\geq 0$, let
\[
\mathsf{Safe}_{n+1}(e)=
\begin{cases}
  \top,
    & e \text{ is a value},\\
  \mathsf{Safe}_n(C[e']),
    & e=C[e_0],\ e_0\rightsquigarrow e',\\
  \operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v)
  \ \land\
  \bigl(\mathsf{Safe}_n(C[r])
    \text{ for }D(\bar v)\text{-almost every }r\bigr),
    & e=C[D^\alpha(\bar v)],\
      \alpha\in\{\G,\E\},\\
  \bot,
    & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
A closed expression $e$ is \emph{domain-safe} if
$\mathsf{Safe}_n(e)$ holds for every $n\in\mathbb N$.
\end{definition}

Domain safety is undecidable in general due to unbounded recursion. We therefore treat it as a semantic assumption, separate from the conditions enforced by our type system.
