\section{Language and Determinization}
\label{sec:language}

This section introduces the probabilistic language on which our determinization transformation operates. We present its syntax and operational semantics, define determinization as a source-to-source transformation over annotated expressions, and formalize the distribution of successfully returned values through an output measure.

\paragraph{Syntax.}
\Cref{fig:syntax} presents the syntax of our language, a call-by-value
functional language with recursion, pairs, sums, lists, and probability distributions. The expression $\kw{reject}$ represents a failed execution path used to encode conditioning on observations.

Each distribution is annotated with one of three modes:
$\G$, $\E$, or $\M$. Both $\G$ and $\E$ draw samples, but $\G$ marks sampling sites that remain stochastic under determinization, whereas $\E$ marks those eligible to be replaced by their means. In particular, the type system in \cref{sec:type-system} determines which sampling sites may be assigned mode $\E$. The mode $\M$, used in the output of determinization, evaluates a distribution expression to its mean.

\input{figures/syntax}

\paragraph{Operational semantics.}
\Cref{fig:semantics} gives the call-by-value operational semantics.
Values and evaluation contexts in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-values}) specify which
expressions have finished evaluating and which subexpression is evaluated
next. In particular, a distribution's arguments are evaluated to values
before it is sampled or its mean is computed.

The deterministic reduction rules in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-reduction}) handle the standard
functional constructs by substitution and case analysis. We omit the
routine arithmetic and comparison rules here. We model the expression $\kw{reject}$ as divergence through the infinite reduction sequence $\kw{reject}\rightsquigarrow\kw{reject}$, and thus our semantics does not distinguish observation failure from nontermination. Distribution expressions in
mode $\M$, introduced by determinization, also reduce deterministically
to their means:
\[
  D^\M(\bar v)
  \rightsquigarrow
  \operatorname{Mean}_D(\bar v)
  \qquad
  \text{when }\operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v),
\]
where the primitive distribution table in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-distributions}) specifies
$\operatorname{Domain}_D$ and $\operatorname{Mean}_D$ for each supported
distribution. A distribution expression whose arguments lie outside its primitive's
domain becomes stuck, as does division by zero: execution cannot take another step or return a value. In $\kw{gaussian}(u,v)$, $v$ denotes
the variance; the parameters $\lambda$ of the exponential and gamma
distributions are rates. The distribution
$\kw{discrete}([p_i]_{i<n})$ returns $i$ with probability $p_i$ for
$0\leq i<n$, and returns $n$ with the remaining probability
$1-\sum_{i<n}p_i$.

In contrast to mode $\M$, modes $\G$ and $\E$ both sample from
$D(\bar v)$ when the arguments satisfy the domain condition $\operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v)$.
Since sampling is stochastic, neither mode has a deterministic reduction
rule. Their identical sampling behavior is instead specified by the
output measure rules in
\cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-output}), which we describe
later in this section.

\paragraph{Determinization} We now define the determinization transformation, with the full inductive definition deferred to \Cref{sec:appendix-determinization}. 

\begin{definition}[Determinization\leanref{determinization}]
\label{def:determinization}
Let $\mathcal{A}=\{\G,\E,\M\}$ be the set of distribution modes. The
determinization transformation $\trans{\cdot}$ is defined structurally on
expressions, changing each $\E$-mode distribution to $\M$-mode and leaving all other constructs unchanged. In particular, for
every distribution $D$, mode $\alpha\in\mathcal{A}$, and argument
expressions $\bar e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$,
\[
\trans{D^\alpha(\bar e)}
=
\begin{cases}
D^\M(\trans{\bar e}) & \text{if } \alpha=\E,\\
D^\alpha(\trans{\bar e}) & \text{if } \alpha\in\{\G,\M\},
\end{cases}
\]
where
\[
\trans{\bar e}
=
(\trans{e_1},\ldots,\trans{e_n}).
\]
For every non-distribution constructor $C$,
\[
\trans{C(e_1,\ldots,e_n)}
=
C(\trans{e_1},\ldots,\trans{e_n}),
\]
and atomic expressions are unchanged.
\end{definition}

Most notably, determinization replaces an $\E$-mode distribution with its mean evaluation and recursively transforms the distribution's arguments, preserving their call-by-value evaluation order. When all distributions are annotated with $\E$, determinization eliminates all randomness, yielding a deterministic program. We refer to this as \emph{full determinization}. When both $\E$- and $\G$-mode distributions occur, determinization eliminates randomness in the former while retaining it in the latter. Accordingly, we refer to this as \emph{partial determinization}. In this case, the resulting program may be discrete or remain continuous, depending on the randomness retained. In particular, if all retained sampling sites are discrete, the transformed program is discrete, even if the original program contains continuous distributions. Conversely, retaining continuous sampling sites may leave the transformed program continuous. Let us consider several simple examples below to illustrate full and partial determinization in action.

\begin{example}[Full determinization]
Consider an $\E$-mode Gaussian whose mean is given by an
$\E$-mode uniform distribution. Determinization changes the
outer Gaussian to $\M$-mode and recursively transforms its
arguments. In particular, the nested $\E$-mode uniform
distribution is also changed to $\M$-mode:
\begin{align*}
\trans{
  \kw{gaussian}^{\E}
    \bigl(\kw{uniform}^{\E}(0,1),1\bigr)}
&=
\kw{gaussian}^{\M}
  \bigl(
    \trans{\kw{uniform}^{\E}(0,1)},
    1
  \bigr)
\\
&=
\kw{gaussian}^{\M}
  \bigl(
    \kw{uniform}^{\M}(0,1),
    1
  \bigr).
\end{align*}
Thus, both sampling sites are replaced by mean evaluations,
yielding a deterministic program.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Partial determinization]
Consider a program with dependent random draws.
The $\G$-mode uniform distribution remains stochastic,
while the $\E$-mode Gaussian is changed to $\M$-mode.
Crucially, the Gaussian's mean $2x+1$ still depends
on the random value of $x$:
\[
\trans{\left(
\begin{aligned}
&\kw{let}\ x=\kw{uniform}^{\G}(0,1)\ \kw{in}\\
&\kw{let}\ y=\kw{gaussian}^{\E}(2x+1,\sigma^2)\ \kw{in}\\
&x*y
\end{aligned}
\right)}
=
\begin{aligned}
&\kw{let}\ x=\kw{uniform}^{\G}(0,1)\ \kw{in}\\
&\kw{let}\ y=\kw{gaussian}^{\M}(2x+1,\sigma^2)\ \kw{in}\\
&x*y.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, determinization eliminates the Gaussian's
conditional randomness while preserving its dependence
on the retained random value of $x$.
\end{example}

We note that determinization itself is syntactic: it performs the required replacements, but the type system is what establishes that those replacements are semantically justified. For example, the type system prevents the $\kw{uniform}$ distribution in
\[
  \kw{let}\ x=\kw{uniform}^{\E}(0,1)\ \kw{in}\ x*x
\]
from being assigned mode $\E$, since for $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$, the original program returns $X^2$, with
expectation $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac{1}{3}$, but replacing $X$ by its mean
instead returns $\mathbb{E}[X]^2=\frac{1}{4}$, changing the expected
returned value. We formalize such constraints in \cref{sec:type-system}.

\paragraph{Output measures.}
To characterize the probabilistic behavior of a program,
we define a subprobability measure over \emph{returned real values}, as shown in \cref{fig:semantics}(\subref{fig:semantics-output}). Let $\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$ denote the space of subprobability measures
on $\mathbb R$, that is, measures on its Borel sets with total mass at
most one, and let $Expr$ be the set of all expressions. For each $n\in\mathbb N$, the function
$\out{(\cdot)}{n}\colon Expr\to\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$
assigns each expression $e$ its output measure after exactly $n$
reduction steps. 
Summing over all finite $n$ defines the function
$\law{(\cdot)}\colon Expr\to\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$,
which assigns each expression its complete output measure.
For each Borel set $B\subseteq\mathbb R$, $\out e n(B)$ and $\law e(B)$
are the probabilities that $e$ returns a value in $B$ after exactly
$n$ steps and after any finite number of steps, respectively.
Since only executions that successfully return a real value
contribute mass, $\law e(\Real)\leq 1$, with the missing mass
accounting for rejection, divergence, or stuck executions.
Thus, $\law e(\Real)$ is precisely the probability that
$e$ successfully returns a real value.

Although executions that reject, diverge, or become stuck contribute no mass to the output measure, stuck executions require special consideration. Replacing a random sample by its mean may change whether a subsequent operation is defined. For example, a uniform distribution whose upper bound depends on a random sample may be undefined for some sampled values but become well-defined after determinization, which can alter the probability of successful return (see~\Cref{sec:appendix-misc}). So to rule out such behavior, we introduce the following
domain safety condition, and henceforth assume that all programs we consider are domain safe.

\begin{definition}[Domain safety\leanref{domain-safety}]
A closed expression $e$ is \emph{domain-safe} if, almost surely,
every executed distribution $D^\alpha(\bar v)$ satisfies
$\operatorname{Domain}_D(\bar v)$ and every executed division
has a nonzero divisor.
\end{definition}
