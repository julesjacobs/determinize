\section{Symbolic Semantics}
\label{sec:proof}

\todo[inline]{Add Lean references}

In this section, we present our proof strategy for establishing the soundness of determinization. The central idea is to introduce a \emph{symbolic operational semantics} that provides a common representation of both the original and determinized programs. We show that both programs can be recovered from the same symbolic execution through two distinct interpretations: one that preserves the original sampling behavior, and another that replaces eligible random choices with their expected values. This correspondence forms the foundation of our soundness argument, which we develop in three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the symbolic semantics;
  \item Introduce the actual and expected interpretations that recover the source and determinized programs, respectively, and establish their correspondence after $n$ reduction steps; and
  \item Extend this $n$ step correspondence to whole-program behavior to derive the tracewise and global soundness theorems.
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:symbolic-semantics}

Let us first introduce the notion of a symbolic state.

\begin{definition}[Symbolic state]
A symbolic state $s \in \mathsf{SymState}$ has the form
\begin{align}
  s &::=(\sigma\mid\widehat e),
  \qquad \widehat e\in\widehat{\mathrm{Expr}},\\
  \sigma
  &::=\emptyset\mid\sigma,\ u\sim D(\bar a),\\
  a
  &=c_0+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_i u_i,
  \qquad c_0,\ldots,c_k\in\Real.
\end{align}
where $a$ is an affine expression\leanref{symbolic-affine} over symbolic variables
$u_1,\ldots,u_k$; $\sigma$ is an ordered symbolic environment\leanref{symbolic-environment} whose declarations
$u\sim D(\bar a)$ associate a fresh variable $u$ with a distribution $D$ containing affine parameters $\bar a$ referring only to earlier variables in $\sigma$;
and $\widehat e$ is a residual symbolic expression\leanref{symbolic-expression}.
\end{definition}

Examining further the two components of $s = (\sigma \mid \hat e)$, first the environment
$\sigma$ in $s$ induces a joint distribution $\mathcal A_\sigma$
over valuations $\rho$ by sampling its declarations in order:
for $\sigma=(u_1\sim D_1(\bar a_1),\ldots,u_k\sim D_k(\bar a_k))$,
\[
  \rho\sim\mathcal A_\sigma:
  \qquad
  \rho(u_i)\sim D_i(\bar a_i[\rho]),
  \quad i=1,\ldots,k.
\]
Each $\bar a_i$ depends only on earlier variables, so this
sequential construction is well-defined for domain-safe
environments. Second, the residual expression $\hat e$ in $s$ retains the source
expression syntax but represents real values using
symbolic literals $\sym a$. This allows computations such as
$\sym2*\sym u+\sym1$ to reduce to $\sym{2u+1}$ without having to sample
$u$ immediately. The typing rules extend those of source expressions as well. The full syntax
and typing rules for residual expressions appear in
\cref{sec:appendix-symbolic-definitions-residual-syntax,sec:appendix-symbolic-definitions-residual-typing}.

We now define how symbolic execution operates on these states. Notably, it proceeds by reducing residual expressions and extending the symbolic environment with declarations for $\E$-mode distributions. \Cref{fig:symbolic-semantics} presents selected rules, with the full semantics given in
\cref{sec:appendix-symbolic-rules}. Residual reductions
$\widehat e_1\rightsquigarrow_{\mathrm{sym}}\widehat e_2$
follow the source reduction rules but operate on symbolic
values, performing arithmetic directly on affine expressions.
In particular, $\operatorname{Mean}^{\#}_D(\bar a)$
represents the mean of $D$ as an affine expression
of its symbolic arguments. The symbolic small-step semantics lifts these reductions
to symbolic states and handles the $\E$- and $\G$-mode distributions additionally.
We distinguish two kinds of symbolic actions:
\[
\underbrace{s\deterministicarrow_{\mathrm{sym}}s'}_{\text{deterministic action}}
\qquad
\underbrace{s\samplingarrow{\mu}_{\mathrm{sym}}k}_{\text{sampling action}}.
\]
Deterministic actions either perform a residual reduction
(\textsc{Reduce}) or record an $\E$-mode draw
(\textsc{Sample-E}). The latter introduces a fresh
symbolic variable $u$, appends the declaration
$u\sim D(\bar a)$ to $\sigma$, and replaces the draw
with $\sym u$.
In contrast, $\G$-mode draws (\textsc{Sample-G}) produce
sampling actions with measure $D(\bar r)$.
More generally, a sampling action consists of a measure
$\mu$ on $\Real$ and a continuation
$k:\Real\to\mathsf{SymState}$ mapping sampled values
to successor states. Its probabilistic meaning is
obtained by integrating the interpreted continuation
against $\mu$. This avoids the need for a measurable structure on symbolic
states, as only the interpreted continuations
must be measurable, as detailed in
\cref{sec:appendix-symbolic-rules}.

\begin{example}[Running example]
\label{ex:symbolic-running}
Consider the following program:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\kw{let}\ x=\kw{uniform}^{\G}(0,1)\ \kw{in}\\
&\kw{let}\ y=\kw{gaussian}^{\E}(x,1)\ \kw{in}\\
&\kw{let}\ z=\kw{gaussian}^{\E}(y,1)\ \kw{in}\\
&x+z.
\end{aligned}
\]
Suppose the $\G$-mode sample produces $x=t$.
Symbolic execution records the two $\E$-mode samples
as fresh variables $u$ and $v$, eventually reaching
the state
\[
\begin{aligned}
  s_t &= (\sigma_t\mid\sym{t+v}),\\
  \sigma_t &=
  \bigl(u\sim\mathcal N(t,1),\
        v\sim\mathcal N(u,1)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\mathcal N$ is a Gaussian distribution. The environment $\sigma_t$ records the dependency of
$v$ on $u$, while the residual expression represents
the returned value without resolving either sample.
In particular, $\sigma_t$ induces the joint distribution
\[
  \mathcal A_{\sigma_t}(du,dv)
  =\mathcal N(t,1)(du)\,\mathcal N(u,1)(dv),
\]
which samples $u$ first and then $v$ conditionally
on $u$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{From source expressions to symbolic states.}
To initialize symbolic execution and relate it to source computations,
we first introduce the following maps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Lifting}\leanref{symbolic-lifting}.
    $\mathsf{lift}:Expr\to\widehat{Expr}$
    embeds a source expression into a residual expression
    by replacing each real literal with a constant affine expression:
    \[
        \mathsf{lift}(r)=\sym{r+\sum_i0u_i}.
    \]

    \item \emph{Realization}\leanref{symbolic-realization}.
    $\mathsf{realize}_\rho:\widehat{Expr}\to Expr$
    maps a residual expression back to a source expression
    by evaluating each symbolic literal under a valuation $\rho$:
    \[
        \mathsf{realize}_\rho
        \left(\sym{c_0+\sum_{i=1}^k c_i u_i}\right)
        =c_0+\sum_{i=1}^k c_i\rho(u_i).
    \]
\end{enumerate}

Both maps otherwise preserve constructors and distribution modes,
acting recursively on subexpressions; their full definitions are given in
\cref{def:appendix-symbolic-maps}. 
Symbolic execution starts from the lifted source expression
with an empty environment:
\[
  \mathsf{init}(e):=(\emptyset\mid\mathsf{lift}(e)).
\]
In particular,
$\mathsf{realize}_{()}(\mathsf{lift}(e))=e$\leanref{symbolic-realize-lift}.


\subsection{Two Interpretations of the Symbolic Semantics}
\label{sec:symbolic-interpretations}
A symbolic state admits two interpretations of its
symbolic environment $\sigma$. The \emph{actual interpretation}
samples the declarations in $\sigma$
according to $\mathcal A_\sigma$, recovering the
randomness of the source computation. The
\emph{expected interpretation} instead assigns each
symbolic variable the mean of its declared distribution
and determinizes the residual expression.  

Since declarations in $\sigma$ may depend on preceding
variables, their means must be evaluated in declaration
order. Specifically, for an environment
$\sigma=(u_1\sim D_1(\bar a_1),\ldots,u_k\sim D_k(\bar a_k))$,
we define the \emph{sequential mean valuation}
$\bar\rho_\sigma$ by
\[
  \bar\rho_\sigma(u_i)
  :=
  \operatorname{Mean}_{D_i}(\bar a_i[\bar\rho_\sigma]),
  \qquad i=1,\ldots,k.
\]
Thus, each variable is assigned the mean of its
declared distribution, evaluated under the assignments
to preceding variables. We can now formally define the two interpretations,
indexed by $n\in\Nat$. As in $\joint e n$, each interpretation records executions
returning after exactly $n$ steps:

\begin{definition}[Actual and expected interpretations]
For a symbolic state $s=(\sigma\mid\widehat e)$, define
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathsf{Act}_n(s)
  &:=
  \int
    \joint{\mathsf{realize}_\rho(\widehat e)}n\,
    \mathcal A_\sigma(d\rho),\\
  \mathsf{Exp}_n(s)
  &:=
  \joint{
    \trans{\mathsf{realize}_{\bar\rho_\sigma}(\widehat e)}
  }n.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{definition}

Both interpretations are measures on $\Trace\times\Real$.
The actual interpretation averages the concrete semantics
over valuations sampled from $\mathcal A_\sigma$, while
the expected interpretation evaluates the residual expression
under the sequential mean valuation and then executes its
determinization.
At initialization, the symbolic environment is empty and
realization recovers the source expression. Hence,
$\mathsf{Act}_n(\mathsf{init}(e))=\joint{e}n$ and
$\mathsf{Exp}_n(\mathsf{init}(e))=\joint{\trans e}n$.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:actual-expected-interpretations}
Returning to \cref{ex:symbolic-running}, the actual
interpretation samples valuations $(u,v)$ according to
$\mathcal A_{\sigma_t}$, while the sequential mean
valuation assigns
\[
  \bar\rho_{\sigma_t}(u)=t,
  \qquad
  \bar\rho_{\sigma_t}(v)=t.
\]
Since $v$ is marginally distributed as
$\mathcal N(t,2)$, the two interpretations
at $n=0$ satisfy, for every measurable
$B\subseteq\Real$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathsf{Act}_0(s_t)(\{[]\}\times B)
  &=\mathcal N(2t,2)(B),\\
  \mathsf{Exp}_0(s_t)(\{[]\}\times B)
  &=\delta_{2t}(B).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here, $n=0$ because the realized residual expression
is already a value. Both measures are concentrated on
the empty trace, since the retained $\G$-mode draw has
already occurred. Thus, the actual interpretation
recovers the randomness represented by the declarations
in $\sigma_t$, whereas the expected interpretation
replaces it with their sequential means.
\end{example}

\subsection{Deriving the Results}

\paragraph{Finite-step factorization.}
Having established that the two interpretations recover the
source and determinized programs, we now relate their output
distributions. The challenge is that the source program may
still sample random values that determinization replaces with
their means. To account for this difference, we introduce a
\emph{trace-indexed output kernel} $K_{n,s}(\tau,\cdot)$,
which characterizes the source output distribution after
$n$ steps, given a fixed trace $\tau$ of retained samples.
The kernel takes $\G$-mode samples from $\tau$ while
integrating over $\E$-mode samples, thereby isolating the
randomness eliminated by determinization. Its formal
definition appears in the appendix.

The key to relating this kernel to the expected interpretation
is the affine structure enforced by our type system.
For any affine expression $a$, its expectation under the
environment distribution $\mathcal A_\sigma$ coincides
with its value under the sequential mean valuation:
\[
  \int a[\rho]\,\mathcal A_\sigma(d\rho)
  = a[\bar\rho_\sigma].
\]
This property allows deferred random choices to be replaced
by their means without changing the expected output,
even when their distributions depend on earlier choices.
Together with the typing restrictions, it gives the
following finite-step correspondence.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-step factorization]
\label{thm:finite-step-factorization}
Let $s=(\sigma\mid\widehat e)$ be a well-typed,
domain-safe symbolic state with $\widehat e:\tyR\E$.
For every $n\in\Nat$ and measurable
$A\subseteq\Trace$, $B\subseteq\Real$,
\[
  \mathsf{Act}_n(s)(A\times B)
  =
  \int_{A\times\Real}
    K_{n,s}(\tau,B)\,
    \mathsf{Exp}_n(s)(d\tau,dr).
\]
Moreover, for $\mathsf{Exp}_n(s)$-almost every
$(\tau,r)$, $K_{n,s}(\tau,\cdot)$ is a probability
measure with finite first absolute moment, and
\[
  r=\int_{\Real}x\,K_{n,s}(\tau,dx).
\]
\end{theorem}

The theorem establishes two complementary properties.
First, the actual interpretation can be reconstructed
from the expected interpretation by restoring the
randomness represented by $K_{n,s}$. Second, the mean
of this reconstructed distribution is precisely the
output of the expected interpretation. Thus,
determinization preserves the expected output
for each retained trace at the level of the
$n$-step semantics.



For \cref{ex:symbolic-running}, the kernel at the
terminal state satisfies
\[
  K_{0,s_t}([],\cdot)=\mathcal N(2t,2),
  \qquad
  \int_{\Real}r\,K_{0,s_t}([],dr)=2t.
\]
Thus, the kernel reconstructs the source output
distribution from the expected interpretation,
whose output is precisely the mean of this distribution.



\paragraph{From finite-step to whole-program soundness.}
The finite-step result relates executions that return
after a fixed number of steps, whereas our soundness
guarantees concern complete program executions.
To bridge this gap, we construct a whole-program
kernel $K_e$ by combining the finite-step kernels
$K_{n,\mathsf{init}(e)}$ across all step counts.
A key compatibility lemma establishes that these
kernels agree with $K_e$ on the relevant successful
traces, ensuring that the finite-step relationship
is preserved when we sum over execution lengths.
The construction and compatibility proof are
given in the appendix.

\begin{theorem}[Whole-program factorization]
\label{thm:whole-program-factorization}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe.
For all measurable $A\subseteq\Trace$ and
$B\subseteq\Real$,
\[
  J_e(A\times B)
  =
  \int_{A\times\Real}
    K_e(\tau,B)\,
    J_{\trans e}(d\tau,dr).
\]
Moreover, for $J_{\trans e}$-almost every
$(\tau,r)$, $K_e(\tau,\cdot)$ is a probability
measure with finite first absolute moment, and
\[
  r=\int_{\Real}x\,K_e(\tau,dx).
\]
\end{theorem}

The whole-program factorization yields tracewise soundness
in two steps: we first identify $K_e$ with the source's
conditional output distribution, then show that the
determinized output is concentrated at its mean.

We now use the whole-program theorem to establish
\cref{thm:trace-soundness}. The key observation is that
the kernel $K_e$ describes the uncertainty in the
source program's output once the retained random choices
have been fixed. To make this precise, we first observe
that the normalization of $K_e$, together with the
whole-program factorization, implies that the source
and determinized programs induce the same trace measure
$\eta$. Relative to this common trace measure, the
factorization identifies $K_e(\tau,\cdot)$ with the
conditional output distribution $\law e(\cdot\mid\tau)$,
for $\eta$-almost every trace $\tau$.
Consequently, the mean of this kernel is precisely
the conditional expected output of the source program:
\[
  m(\tau)
  :=\int_\Real x\,K_e(\tau,dx)
  =\Expect[e\mid\tau].
\]

The second part of the whole-program theorem
characterizes the output of the determinized program.
It guarantees that, for almost every execution of
$\trans e$, the returned value equals $m(\tau)$,
where $\tau$ is the retained trace of that execution.
In other words, once the retained random choices are
fixed, the determinized program has no remaining
uncertainty in its returned value. Its conditional
output distribution is therefore a point mass at
$m(\tau)$:
\[
  \law{\trans e}(\cdot\mid\tau)
  =\delta_{m(\tau)}
  =\delta_{\Expect[e\mid\tau]}.
\]
This establishes \cref{thm:trace-soundness}:
determinization preserves the distribution of retained
traces while replacing, for almost every such trace,
the source's conditional output distribution with a
point mass at its mean. The full measure-theoretic
derivation is given in \cref{sec:appendix-trace-soundness}.

To illustrate, consider again the program from
\cref{ex:symbolic-running}. For a retained trace
$\tau_t=[(\kw{uniform},t)]$, the whole-program kernel
is $K_e(\tau_t,\cdot)=\mathcal N(2t,2)$.
Tracewise soundness therefore gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \law e(\cdot\mid\tau_t)
  &=\mathcal N(2t,2),\\
  \law{\trans e}(\cdot\mid\tau_t)
  &=\delta_{2t}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, the Gaussian uncertainty remaining after
fixing the uniform sample is eliminated, while
the randomness of the uniform sample itself is
preserved. Both programs consequently have expected
output $1$, but determinization reduces the output
variance from $7/3$ to $1/3$.
