\section{Symbolic Semantics}
\label{sec:symbolic-semantics}

In this section, we present our proof strategy for establishing the soundness of determinization, using the source program from \cref{fig:intro-signal} in \Cref{sec:introduction} as a running example to illustrate the main ideas. The central idea is to introduce a \emph{symbolic operational semantics} that provides a common representation of both the original and determinized programs. Instead of immediately sampling $\E$-mode
distributions, symbolic execution records these choices and postpones their evaluation. Two interpretations of the resulting states then recover the source program and its determinization.

\paragraph{Symbolic execution}
Let us first introduce the notion of a symbolic state.

\begin{definition}[Symbolic state]
A symbolic state $s$ has the form
\begin{align*}
  s &::=(\sigma\mid\widehat e),
  \qquad \widehat e\in\widehat{\mathrm{Expr}},\\
  \sigma &::=\emptyset\mid\sigma,\ u\sim D(\bar a),\\
  a &=c_0+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_i u_i,
  \qquad c_0,\ldots,c_k\in\Real.
\end{align*}
Here, $\widehat e$ is a residual symbolic expression
\leanref{symbolic-expression}, whose real literals may
be affine expressions $a$\leanref{symbolic-affine}.
The symbolic environment $\sigma$\leanref{symbolic-environment}
is an ordered sequence of declarations
$u\sim D(\bar a)$, where $u$ is fresh and the
parameters $\bar a$ may refer only to earlier
variables in $\sigma$.
\end{definition}

Consider the running example, which
samples $x$ from a uniform distribution and then
samples two dependent Gaussian variables $y$ and $z$
before returning $xz$.
Suppose the $\G$-mode sample produces $x=t$.
Symbolic execution records the Gaussian samples as
fresh variables $u$ and $v$, eventually reaching the terminal state shown in \cref{fig:symbolic-example}:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  s_t &= (\sigma_t\mid\sym{tv}), \qquad
  \sigma_t &=
  \bigl(u\sim\mathcal N(t,1),\
        v\sim\mathcal N(u,1)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
The environment $\sigma_t$ records the dependence of $v$ on $u$
without sampling either variable. The residual
expression $\sym{tv}$ is affine in $v$, since the
retained sample $t$ has already been fixed.

Each environment $\sigma$ induces a joint distribution
$\mathcal A_\sigma$\leanref{symbolic-actual-environment}
over valuations $\rho$ by sampling its declarations
in order: for
$\sigma=(u_i\sim D_i(\bar a_i))_{i=1}^k$,
the distribution $\mathcal A_\sigma$ samples
$\rho(u_i)\sim D_i(\bar a_i[\rho])$ sequentially. For this example,
\[
  \mathcal A_{\sigma_t}(du,dv)
  =\mathcal N(t,1)(du)\,\mathcal N(u,1)(dv).
\]

We now define how symbolic execution operates on these symbolic states. Notably, it proceeds by reducing residual expressions and extending the symbolic environment with declarations for $\E$-mode distributions. \Cref{fig:symbolic-semantics} gives the symbolic
reduction rules. Ordinary reductions operate on
symbolic expressions, while an $\E$-mode draw adds
a declaration to $\sigma$ (\textsc{Sample-E}).
A $\G$-mode draw instead samples a concrete value
(\textsc{Sample-G}), as in the uniform sample above.
Thus, symbolic execution samples only the choices
that determinization retains. The full rules are
given in \cref{sec:appendix-symbolic-rules}.

\subsection{Two Interpretations of the Symbolic Semantics}
\label{sec:symbolic-interpretations}

A symbolic state can be interpreted in two ways.
The \emph{actual interpretation} samples the
declarations in $\sigma$ according to
$\mathcal A_\sigma$, recovering the source program's
behavior. The \emph{expected interpretation} instead
replaces each symbolic variable by the mean of its
declared distribution.

Since distributions may depend on earlier variables,
these means are evaluated sequentially. For
$\sigma=(u_i\sim D_i(\bar a_i))_{i=1}^k$,
define the \emph{sequential mean valuation}
$\bar\rho_\sigma$\leanref{symbolic-mean-environment} by
\[
  \bar\rho_\sigma(u_i)
  :=\operatorname{Mean}_{D_i}
       (\bar a_i[\bar\rho_\sigma]),
  \qquad i=1,\ldots,k.
\]
In our example, this gives
$\bar\rho_{\sigma_t}(u)=t$ and
$\bar\rho_{\sigma_t}(v)=t$.
Thus, while the actual interpretation samples
$v$ according to its dependent Gaussian distribution,
the expected interpretation replaces it by $t$,
making the returned value $t^2$, as shown in the two interpretation boxes in \cref{fig:symbolic-example}.

To define these interpretations formally, we use
two maps. Lifting $\mathsf{lift}$\leanref{symbolic-lifting} embeds a source
expression into a residual expression, replacing
each real literal $r$ with $\sym{r}$.
Realization $\mathsf{realize}_\rho$\leanref{symbolic-realization} evaluates
symbolic literals under a valuation $\rho$:
\[
  \mathsf{realize}_\rho(\sym{a})=a[\rho].
\]
Both maps otherwise preserve expression constructors.
Symbolic execution begins at
$\mathsf{init}(e):=(\emptyset\mid\mathsf{lift}(e))$,
and $\mathsf{realize}_{()}(\mathsf{lift}(e))=e$\leanref{symbolic-realize-lift}.
The full definitions appear in
\cref{def:appendix-symbolic-maps}.

\begin{definition}[Actual and expected interpretations\leanref{symbolic-actual-interpretation}\leanref{symbolic-expected-interpretation}]
For $s=(\sigma\mid\widehat e)$ and $n\in\Nat$, define
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathsf{Act}_n(s)
  &:=
  \int
    \joint{\mathsf{realize}_\rho(\widehat e)}n\,
    \mathcal A_\sigma(d\rho),\\
  \mathsf{Exp}_n(s)
  &:=
  \joint{
    \trans{\mathsf{realize}_{\bar\rho_\sigma}(\widehat e)}
  }n.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{definition}

Both interpretations are measures on
$\Trace\times\Real$, recording executions that
return after exactly $n$ remaining steps.
At initialization, they recover the source and determinized programs: $
  \mathsf{Act}_n(\mathsf{init}(e)) = \joint{e}n$ and 
  $\mathsf{Exp}_n(\mathsf{init}(e)) = \joint{\trans e}n$. Returning to $s_t$, the actual interpretation
samples $u$ and $v$, while the expected
interpretation substitutes their sequential means.
Since $v$ has marginal distribution
$\mathcal N(t,2)$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathsf{Act}_0(s_t)(\{[]\}\times\cdot)
    &=\mathcal N(t^2,2t^2),\\
  \mathsf{Exp}_0(s_t)(\{[]\}\times\cdot)
    &=\delta_{t^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The two interpretations have different output distributions, but the mean
under the actual interpretation equals the value returned by the expected
interpretation. This equality follows from the affine-means identity
(\cref{lem:appendix-affine-means}): for a well-typed, domain-safe
symbolic environment $\sigma$ and affine
expression $a$,
\[
  \int a[\rho]\,\mathcal A_\sigma(d\rho)
  =a[\bar\rho_\sigma].\leanref{symbolic-affine-expectation}
\]
In particular, the equality holds even when
the distributions in $\sigma$ depend on
earlier samples. Together with the typing
restrictions, the affine-means identity relates the actual and expected
interpretations throughout symbolic execution.

\subsection{Deriving the Soundness Results}

\paragraph{Finite-step factorization.}
To relate the two interpretations, we introduce
a trace-indexed output kernel
$K_{n,s}(\tau,\cdot)$\leanref{trace-indexed-output-kernel}.
Given a trace $\tau$ of $\G$-mode
samples, the kernel describes the source
output distribution after $n$ remaining steps,
integrating over the $\E$-mode samples.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-step factorization
\leanref{finite-step-factorization}]
\label{thm:finite-step-factorization}
Let $s=(\sigma\mid\widehat e)$ be a well-typed,
domain-safe symbolic state with $\widehat e:\tyR\E$.
For every $n\in\Nat$ and measurable
$A\subseteq\Trace$, $B\subseteq\Real$,
\[
  \mathsf{Act}_n(s)(A\times B)
  =
  \int_{A\times\Real}
    K_{n,s}(\tau,B)\,
    \mathsf{Exp}_n(s)(d\tau,dr).
\]
Moreover, for $\mathsf{Exp}_n(s)$-almost every
$(\tau,r)$, $K_{n,s}(\tau,\cdot)$ is a probability
measure with finite first absolute moment, and
\[
  r=\int_{\Real}x\,K_{n,s}(\tau,dx).
\]
\end{theorem}

The first equality recovers the actual
interpretation by restoring the randomness
removed by the expected interpretation.
The mean identity states that the determinized
output is the mean of $K_{n,s}(\tau,\cdot)$.
For our terminal state, for example,
\[
  K_{0,s_t}([],\cdot)=\mathcal N(t^2,2t^2),
  \qquad
  \int_\Real r\,K_{0,s_t}([],dr)=t^2.
\]

\paragraph{From finite steps to whole programs.}
We extend finite-step factorization (\cref{thm:finite-step-factorization}) by combining the
kernels $K_{n,\mathsf{init}(e)}$ over all possible
return times. A compatibility lemma\leanref{replay-compatibility} ensures that
the resulting whole-program kernel $K_e$\leanref{whole-program-output-kernel} preserves
the factorization when summing over step counts\leanref{whole-program-kernel-factorization}.

\begin{theorem}[Whole-program factorization
\leanref{whole-program-factorization}]
\label{thm:whole-program-factorization}
Suppose $\vdash e:\tyR\E$ and $e$ is domain-safe.
For all measurable $A\subseteq\Trace$ and
$B\subseteq\Real$,
\[
  J_e(A\times B)
  =
  \int_{A\times\Real}
    K_e(\tau,B)\,
    J_{\trans e}(d\tau,dr).
\]
Moreover, for $J_{\trans e}$-almost every
$(\tau,r)$, $K_e(\tau,\cdot)$ is a probability
measure with finite first absolute moment, and
\[
  r=\int_{\Real}x\,K_e(\tau,dx).
\]
\end{theorem}

Since $K_e$ is a probability measure on
successful traces, the factorization implies
that the source and determinized programs have
the same trace measure\leanref{trace-preservation}. Moreover,
$K_e(\tau,\cdot)$ is the source's conditional
output distribution $\law e(\cdot\mid\tau)$,
whose mean is
\[
  m(\tau)
  :=\int_\Real x\,K_e(\tau,dx)
  =\Expect[e\mid\tau].
\]
The mean identity in the whole-program factorization theorem
(\cref{thm:whole-program-factorization}) shows that
the determinized program returns $m(\tau)$
for almost every retained trace. Therefore,
\[
  \law{\trans e}(\cdot\mid\tau)
  =\delta_{m(\tau)}
  =\delta_{\Expect[e\mid\tau]},
\]
establishing tracewise soundness
(\cref{thm:trace-soundness})\leanref{trace-soundness}.

For the running example, fixing the retained
trace $\tau_t=[(\kw{uniform},t)]$ gives
\[
  \law e(\cdot\mid\tau_t)
    =\mathcal N(t^2,2t^2),
  \qquad
  \law{\trans e}(\cdot\mid\tau_t)
    =\delta_{t^2}.
\]
Integrating over $t\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$,
both programs have expected output $1/3$,
while determinization removes the conditional
Gaussian variance. More generally, integrating over the common
trace measure gives expectation preservation
whenever the global expectation is well-defined\leanref{expectation-preservation}.
The law of total variance then gives variance
non-increase when the second moment is finite\leanref{variance-non-increase},
establishing the global soundness theorems in
\cref{sec:soundness}.
