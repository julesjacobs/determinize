\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Probabilistic programs describe computations that make random choices, yet many analyses seek only the expected return, such as the average reward, cost, or outcome across executions. Monte Carlo methods estimate this expectation by repeatedly executing the program and averaging its outputs. However, the precision of these estimates depends on the variability of the outputs, with higher variance requiring more samples. This motivates a natural alternative: eliminating random choices whose effects can be accounted for analytically, without changing the expected return.

Existing approaches exploit this idea in different ways. Exact inference and moment analysis compute expectations symbolically~\cite{gehr2016psi,gehr2020lpsi,moosbrugger2022moment}. Conditional Monte Carlo and Rao--Blackwellization reduce sampling variance by replacing random quantities with their conditional expectations~\cite{casella1996rao,owen2013montecarlo}. Related techniques combine symbolic reasoning with sampling during inference~\cite{murray2018delayed,atkinson2022semisymbolic}, while expectation programming targets the estimation of expected returns directly~\cite{reichelt2022expectation}. These techniques suggest that not every random choice needs to be explicitly simulated. However, identifying which choices can be safely eliminated is challenging: random values may influence control flow, determine the parameters of subsequent distributions, or participate in nonlinear computations. This raises a natural question:
\begin{center}
\emph{Can we statically identify and eliminate random choices from a probabilistic program while preserving its expected return?}
\end{center}

Consider the signal and noisy measurement program in
\cref{fig:intro-signal}. The program samples a signal $x$ uniformly
from $[0,1]$, samples a Gaussian measurement $y$ with mean $x$,
and returns their product $xy$. Once $x$ is fixed, the measurement
noise contributes nothing to the average product:
$$
  \mathbb E[xy\mid x]
  =x\,\mathbb E[y\mid x]
  =x^2.
$$
Replacing the Gaussian sample by its mean therefore yields a simpler
program that samples only $x$ and returns $x^2$. Both programs have
expected return $1/3$, but the transformed program avoids generating
measurement noise and thus has lower output variance.

\input{figures/intro-signal}

This replacement also reveals the central difficulty. Replacing the
remaining uniform sample by its mean, $1/2$, would make the program
return $1/4$ rather than $1/3$. One random choice can be removed;
the other must remain. The distinction depends on how their values
are used: the returned product is linear in $y$ when $x$ is fixed,
but becomes quadratic in $x$ after averaging over $y$. A sound
transformation must therefore reason about both the dependencies
between random choices and their uses throughout the program. This challenge is particularly pronounced in higher-order, potentially unbounded recursive programs, where the influence of a random choice can propagate through function calls and across arbitrarily long executions. 

Our key insight is that safe replacements can nevertheless be identified compositionally from how sampled values are used, without requiring symbolic marginalization of their distributions. We introduce \emph{determinization}, a type-directed,
source-to-source transformation of higher-order, recursive probabilistic programs with continuous distributions that automatically identifies
which samples can be replaced by their means. Our type system
distinguishes sampling choices that remain random ($\G$) from
those that can be eliminated ($\E$). Type inference determines
these modes by tracking how sampled values are used: values
marked $\E$ may participate in affine computations, but cannot
determine control flow or the parameters of retained samples.
The transformation then replaces each $\E$-mode sample by its
primitive mean. The resulting program preserves the expected
return while potentially changing the output distribution.

The next three examples illustrate how determinization handles
higher-order functions, data structures, and unbounded recursion.
We begin with the Gaussian random walk in
\cref{fig:intro-gaussian-walk}, from~\cite{vakar2019domain}.
The program passes a probabilistic step function to
\texttt{reduce}, a left fold that traverses the requested times
and accumulates a list of time-position pairs (the full program, including the definition
of \texttt{reduce}, appears in~\cref{sec:appendix-gauss-random-walk}).
At each time $t'$, it samples a new position from
$\mathcal N(y,t'-t)$, where $(t,y)$ is the latest pair in the path. It then adds $(t',y')$ to the front of the path. At the next step, it reads that pair and uses $y'$ as the next Gaussian's mean. Inference marks the Gaussian sampling
site as determinizable without unrolling the traversal.
Replacing the draws by their means keeps every position at zero,
yielding a deterministic path that matches the expected position
at each requested time.

Beyond the transformation itself, determinization can improve
Monte Carlo estimation of expected returns.
The dungeon crawl in \cref{fig:intro-dungeon} recursively visits
rooms until an exit coin ends the computation, with no fixed
bound on the number of rooms visited.
At depth $d$, a room pays a jackpot of $200d$ with probability
$0.01$. The exit coins remain random because they control
recursion, while each jackpot contributes linearly to the total
reward and is replaced by its mean payout, $2d$.
This preserves the expected return while reducing output
variance by a factor of $12.36$ (\cref{fig:intro-variance}).
Since the variance of an average of $N$ independent runs is
the output variance divided by $N$, the transformed program
requires correspondingly fewer samples to achieve the same
estimator variance.

Moreover, removing randomness can do more than improve sampling.
In \cref{fig:intro-exact}, a recursive process either returns
its current state $x$ or continues from $1-x$ plus Gaussian
noise, each with probability $1/2$. Determinization removes
the noise: starting from zero, the transformed loop has only
two possible states, $0$ and $1$. Their expected returns satisfy
$$
  v_0=\tfrac12v_1,
  \qquad
  v_1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12v_0,
$$
giving $v_0=1/3$. A continuous-state computation thus becomes
a finite-state problem whose expectation can be computed
exactly. Our implementation supports this analysis with
certificate checking for the resulting exact values.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Type system and inference.}
  We develop a type system and source-to-source transformation
  that replace eligible samples by their primitive means in
  higher-order recursive programs.
  Our inference algorithm finds the greatest assignment of
  determinizable sites permitted by the type system.

  \item \textbf{Soundness.} We prove that determinization preserves the
    expectation value and only decreases variance. 

    \item \textbf{Lean implementation and verification.} We implement type
    inference, determinization, and sampling in Lean, and mechanize our
    soundness theorems. The full Lean code is available at [TODO], references to individual parts are indicated by a superscript Lean logo like this\leanmark. When determinization yields a finite-state program,
    verified matrix solving additionally computes its expectation value exactly.
    This equals the source expectation under the source safety and integrability
    hypotheses of our preservation theorem.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation.} We examine the transformation's ability to
  enable exact analysis, reduce output variance, and improve
  the precision of Monte Carlo estimates for a given time budget.
\end{itemize}
